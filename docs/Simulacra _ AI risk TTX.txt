14/11/25_bhishma
The challenge of artificial general intelligence (AGI) demands more than abstract theory or wishful thinking; it requires concrete tools that address the fundamental problem of concentrated power. That is the main motion driving Simulacra. My high-level vision isn't just to fix AI, but to confront the central risk that these models will gradually take over and run our economy, resulting in a systemic gradual disempowerment [1]. People will welcome it because they are local optimizers, and the economy rewards that optimization, but unchecked power leads to autocracy, whether political or technological [2]. We are already deep into a technofeudalist society, and AGI risks making that worse, dictated by a few shareholders [3].


History gives us a clear lesson: stable governance requires balancing power, drawing the best from monarchy (executive), oligarchy (aristocracy/shareholders), and democracy (the people) [3]. We need to disseminate a nuanced understanding of these systemic risks to all these sections—the monarchs, the oligarchs, and the democracy—or we risk failure [3, 4].
Why Static Education Fails
Reading a policy paper or watching a documentary is insufficient. Static education struggles with customization and audience biases [4]. My core belief is that to understand complex, non-linear systems, you need to experience them [5]. This isn't theoretical for me. I realized the supremacy of a strong economy over raw military might, not from some economist's brief, but from playing Age of Empires (AoE). The consequence of neglecting my economy was vivid, immediate, and burnt into my brain because I faced the hardship of losing [6-8].


Simulacra is built to provide that exact form of experiential learning. It functions as a "living lab" where players can try things and see the consequences, making mistakes in a safe environment and learning the true causal connections between actions and outcomes [5, 9, 10]. This connects directly to the contemporary concepts of AI safety, where large language models (LLMs) are viewed as simulators that instantiate complex, goal-driven simulacra (the agent personas) based on the input prompt [6].
Current Mechanics and Potential
Currently, our Minimum Viable Product (MVP) uses a very light LLM (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite) to prototype predicting behaviors in multi-stakeholder scenarios [5, 11]. The game is a sequential multi-agent scenario constrained to a finite number of rounds [12]. The strategic tension relies on a system of Dual Objectives [13, 14]:


      1. Shared Objective (Public Score): A metric like Democratic Legitimacy or Environmental Safety that everyone must maintain above zero, or the entire group suffers catastrophic failure [13, 15].
      2. Hidden Objective (Private Score): A win condition specific to that role (e.g., maximizing profit, passing a specific bill), often conflicting with the public good [13, 14].


This structure immediately forces players into a principal-agent dilemma, navigating the collective good against conflicting individual incentives [14]. We constrain human decision-making—players receive a limited number of Action Points (AP) per round and select from a finite set of options generated by the AI Game Master [16, 17]. The fact that the LLM, given a scenario prompt, can identify complex stakeholders and generate non-obvious options—such as securing intellectual property or controlling the supply chain for a pharmaceutical conglomerate—is already fascinating and proves its potential [16, 18]. It exposes players to possibilities that didn't exist in their initial purview [19, 20]. This ability to see options outside your own training or biases is what fundamentally challenges your world model [21].


A critical learning tool we built in is the Counterfactual Analysis: after each round, we explicitly show the outcome that would have occurred had no agents acted [17, 22]. This gives players an immediate benchmark for assessing the value (or cost) of their intervention [17].
The Need for Rigorous Architecture and Scale
While the LLM is powerful for narrative and context, relying entirely on implicit probabilistic definitions for the state transition function makes the system hard to control and less rigorous [11, 23, 24]. To achieve the level of complexity and exploitability required for sustained learning—what I call skill progression—we must formalize the engine [25].


This requires transitioning to a Hybrid Simulation Architecture [26] that marries the LLM’s creativity with deterministic modeling components:


      1. System Dynamics: Mapping the system using stocks (things that accumulate, like money, trust, or population) and flows (things that increase or decrease those stocks) [27, 28]. This allows us to model classic evolutionary dynamics and the positive and negative feedback loops that define complex systems [29, 30]. This structure gives the game a predictable, rich foundation that players can learn and exploit, transitioning the game from a random event generator to a system where players can genuinely improve their skill [25, 31].
      2. Agent-Based Modeling (ABM): Formalizing the AI stakeholders as autonomous agents seeking to maximize their dual utility (public and hidden score) [32, 33]. We are exploring methods where the LLM itself could write the necessary Python or JavaScript code for these models based on domain knowledge and library documentation [34].


Crucially, this architectural refactoring is about making Simulacra a generalized Toolbox and Workbench, not a single product like "Intelligence Rising" (which I view as a product, not a tool) [35, 36]. The core structure—the mechanics of turns, action points, and dual objectives—must remain fixed, but the scenario content—roles, specific metrics like "CO2 ppm," narrative events—must be entirely configurable [37, 38]. This allows anyone to design a new scenario, applying the engine to technology, climate, public health, or geopolitical crises [37, 39].
Priorities: Aesthetics, Agents, and Experience
We need to flip the priority structure from the standard engineering approach of Mechanics-Dynamics-Aesthetics (MDA). My view is that we must focus on Aesthetics, Dynamics, then Mechanics (ADM) [40]. Aesthetics—how the game makes the user feel—is the key lever for engagement and learning [40, 41]. The UI/UX design must be clean and remove clutter, offering tailored views for different roles (a Tech CEO sees stock price, a Regulator sees a complaint index) [41, 42].


For immediate high-impact iterations, our focus is on two key areas:


      1. True Agentic Behavior: Our AI opponents need to move beyond simple LLM calls and become agents that can call tools, talk to other agents, debate, negotiate, and simulate complex human behaviors like deception and lying [43, 44]. We should aim to cross a Turing test where players cannot distinguish between AI opponents and human opponents [44].
      2. Multiplayer Implementation: The deepest learning—the true messiness of politics—comes from human-to-human interaction, not just human-to-AI [45, 46]. We are designing for asynchronous multiplayer, including in-game communication channels, such as email threads between rounds, where players can negotiate, form alliances, or deceive each other [45, 47].


Finally, we need feedback. Simply writing blog posts hasn't worked [5]. We need capital to facilitate in-person trials where we can get live feedback, push edits, and truly understand what direction the prototype should take to maximize impact—be it education, strategy, or policy simulation [5]. We need to confirm that the game is indeed causing people to change their views or generate new ideas, such as moving past a simplistic "China bad" view because they played the role of China and realized the complexity [21, 48]. This qualitative and quantitative feedback is essential to capitalize on the substantial headroom this platform offers [49-51].
