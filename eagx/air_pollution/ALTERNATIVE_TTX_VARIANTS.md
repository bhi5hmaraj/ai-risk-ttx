# Alternative TTX Variants: Beyond Personal vs Public Goals

## Overview

This document explores alternative game mechanics and pedagogical approaches for tabletop exercises that go beyond the standard "personal hidden objective vs public goal" tension. We examine different frameworks for creating engaging, educational TTX experiences that teach complex systems, coordination challenges, and policy trade-offs.

---

## Variant 1: **Role-Based Epistemic Asymmetry**

### Core Mechanic
Instead of hidden objectives, each player has **exclusive information** and **unique capabilities**.

### Structure
- **Information Fragmentation**: Each role sees different data
  - Health Director: Sees real hospitalization numbers
  - Weather Chief: Sees PM2.5 dispersion forecasts
  - Farmer Rep: Knows actual burning intentions (ABM agent data)
  - Industry Leader: Knows compliance rates
  - Activist: Sees public sentiment trends

- **Asymmetric Action Spaces**: Each role can do things others can't
  - Only Environment Minister can authorize inter-state subsidies
  - Only Chief Minister can declare emergency (GRAP Stage 4)
  - Only Health Director can close schools
  - Only Activist can trigger media events

### Learning Objectives
- **Information aggregation under uncertainty**
- **Communication strategies** (what to share, what to withhold)
- **Distributed decision-making**
- **Value of transparency vs strategic ambiguity**

### Example Round Flow
1. Each player receives private briefing (different information)
2. Deliberation: Players share (or don't) their information
3. Action selection: Some actions require consensus (vote), others unilateral
4. Consequence: Reveal how partial information led to suboptimal outcomes
5. Debrief: "What if everyone had shared everything?"

### Advantages
- More realistic (real policymaking is information-fragmented)
- Teaches communication skills
- Natural emergence of leader/coordinator roles
- No "hidden objectives guilt" (not lying, just have different info)

### Challenges
- Requires careful info distribution (complex setup)
- Risk of "kingmaker" (one player has critical info)
- Hard to balance (some info more valuable than others)

## Variant 2: **Temporal Dilemmas & Long-term Thinking**

### Core Mechanic
Actions have **delayed effects** and **path dependencies**. Players must balance short-term crises with long-term investments.

### Structure
- **Three Time Horizons**:
  - **Immediate (this round)**: Emergency measures (e.g., vehicle ban)
  - **Medium (2-3 rounds)**: Capacity building (e.g., farmer training programs)
  - **Long-term (5+ rounds, persist after game)**: Infrastructure (e.g., metro expansion)

- **Delayed Gratification**:
  - Metro expansion: Costs ₹500 cr in Round 1, reduces AQI by 5 in Round 3, by 15 in Round 5
  - Farmer cooperatives: Costs ₹200 cr in Round 1-2, enables non-burning alternatives by Round 4
  - Industrial tech upgrades: Costs ₹300 cr, reduces emissions by 30% after 2 rounds

- **Crisis-Punishment Cycles**:
  - If you don't address root causes, crises recur with increasing severity
  - Reinforcing loop: Crisis → Emergency measures → Neglect long-term → Worse crisis

### Learning Objectives
- **Discounting** (present vs future value)
- **Path dependence** (earlier choices constrain later options)
- **Tragedy of short-termism** (political cycles vs policy timescales)
- **Compound effects** (small investments accumulate)

### Example Round Flow
1. Crisis event (AQI spike)
2. Players face choice: Emergency response (quick fix) OR long-term investment
3. If choose emergency: AQI drops this round, but baseline unchanged
4. If choose investment: No immediate relief, but future benefits
5. Debrief: Compare short-term vs long-term players' final outcomes

### Advantages
- Deeply educational (hardest concept in policy: delayed effects)
- Encourages systems thinking
- Realistic (climate, infrastructure, education all have long lags)

### Challenges
- Frustrating for players (action in Round 1 pays off in Round 5)
- Needs 7-10 rounds to show compounding effects
- Risk of early "give up" if crisis feels hopeless

### Design Solutions
- **Make future visible**: Show projections of what delayed actions will do
- **Partial effects**: Long-term actions have small immediate benefits + large delayed benefits
- **Hope mechanics**: Introduce "positive spiral" events if invest long-term

## Variant 3: **Negotiation & Coalition Formation**

### Core Mechanic
Players must **form coalitions** to pass policies. No single player can act alone.

### Structure
- **Voting System**:
  - Each action requires X votes to pass
  - Veto powers (Farmer Rep can veto stubble ban, Industry can veto emission controls)
  - Budget allocation requires majority

- **Coalition Incentives**:
  - Policies have **distribution effects** (who pays, who benefits)
  - Example: Farmer subsidy benefits farmers, costs urban taxpayers
  - Players must negotiate transfers: "I'll support your subsidy if you support my metro"

- **Bargaining Rounds**:
  - Pre-action phase: 10 minutes for negotiations
  - Players can make binding commitments
  - Side payments allowed (political capital, budget allocations)

### Learning Objectives
- **Collective action problems**
- **Distributive politics** (who gets what)
- **Coalition building** (minimum winning coalitions)
- **Logrolling** (vote trading)

### Example Round Flow
1. Proposals generated (3-5 policy bundles)
2. Negotiation phase: Players cut deals
3. Voting: Each player reveals vote
4. If passed: Implement action, distribute costs/benefits
5. If failed: No action, status quo continues
6. Debrief: Why did coalition form or fail?

### Advantages
- Highly interactive (social game, not solo optimization)
- Teaches negotiation skills
- Realistic (real politics is coalitional)
- Fun (players love wheeling and dealing)

### Challenges
- Requires strong facilitation (negotiations can stall)
- Risk of dominant player steamrolling
- Time-intensive (deliberation takes 15-20 min)

### Design Solutions
- **Structured negotiation**: Time limits, rotating chair
- **Secret ballots**: Prevent intimidation
- **Pre-designed coalitions**: Give players suggested alliances

## Variant 4: **Mechanism Design Sandbox**

### Core Mechanic
Players **design the rules** of the game mid-game. Meta-level: choosing incentive structures.

### Structure
- **Phase 1 (Rounds 1-2)**: Play standard game, experience coordination failure
- **Phase 2 (Round 3)**: PAUSE. Players collectively design a new rule/mechanism
  - Examples:
    - Carbon tax on farmers
    - Subsidy matching (if 3 players contribute, 4th gets bonus)
    - Information sharing protocol
    - Quota system (tradable permits)
    - Binding agreements (contracts)
- **Phase 3 (Rounds 4-5)**: Play with new rules

### Learning Objectives
- **Mechanism design** (how rules shape behavior)
- **Incentive alignment** (design to achieve goals)
- **Institutional innovation**
- **Comparative institutions** (which mechanisms work best?)

### Example Round Flow
1. Rounds 1-2: Players struggle with coordination (experience failure)
2. Pause: "You're designers now. What rule would help?"
3. Deliberation: Players propose mechanisms (vote on one to implement)
4. Rounds 3-5: Play with new mechanism
5. Debrief: Did it work? Why/why not? What would you change?

### Advantages
- Unique (meta-level gameplay)
- Teaches mechanism design (advanced econ concept)
- Empowering (players as designers)
- Reusable (different groups try different mechanisms)

### Challenges
- Complex (requires understanding of game theory)
- Risk of "broken" mechanisms (players design bad rules)
- Time-intensive (need facilitator to implement new rules)

### Design Solutions
- **Mechanism library**: Provide menu of pre-tested mechanisms
- **Constraints**: Can only change X, Y, Z parameters
- **Simulation preview**: Show players what mechanism would do (run counterfactual)

## Variant 5: **Crisis Response Under Uncertainty**

### Core Mechanic
**Radical uncertainty**. Players don't know the model. They must experiment and learn.

### Structure
- **Black Box Model**: Players see inputs (actions) and outputs (AQI) but not the causal model
- **Experimentation Phase**: Players can "test" actions (small-scale pilots) before committing
- **Bayesian Learning**: As players act, they update beliefs about how the system works

- **Hidden Dynamics**:
  - Non-linear effects (subsidy effectiveness drops if adoption >60%)
  - Threshold effects (AQI spikes if temperature <15°C + stubble burning)
  - Unknown externalities (factory closure leads to job loss → political unrest)

### Learning Objectives
- **Learning under uncertainty**
- **Experimentation** (explore-exploit trade-off)
- **Model-building** (reverse-engineering causality)
- **Robustness** (strategies that work across scenarios)

### Example Round Flow
1. Players receive observations (AQI, events) but no causal explanations
2. Deliberation: "What do we think is happening?"
3. Action: Players choose, unsure of effects
4. Consequence: Reveal outcome (but not full mechanism)
5. Players update mental models
6. Repeat
7. Debrief: "What did you learn? How did you learn it?"

### Advantages
- Mimics real-world uncertainty (policymakers don't have perfect models)
- Encourages scientific thinking (hypothesis → test → update)
- High replay value (different games → different learning curves)

### Challenges
- Frustrating (players hate uncertainty)
- Risk of learned helplessness ("nothing we do matters")
- Requires patient players

### Design Solutions
- **Gradual revelation**: Provide hints after Round 2
- **Experimentation budget**: Give players "pilot program" action (cheap, reveals info)
- **Confidence intervals**: Show "likely range" of effects

## Variant 6: **Multi-Stakeholder Simulation (No Hidden Objectives)**

### Core Mechanic
Fully **transparent preferences**, but goals are **incommensurable**.

### Structure
- All objectives are public, but fundamentally different:
  - Health Director: Minimize DALYs lost
  - Farmer Rep: Maximize farmer income
  - Chief Minister: Maximize re-election probability
  - Activist: Maximize air quality equality (reduce gap between rich/poor areas)
  - Industry: Maximize GDP growth

- **No single metric**: Can't convert health to money to votes
- **Pareto Trade-offs**: Any policy helps some, hurts others

### Learning Objectives
- **Value pluralism** (multiple valid goals)
- **Pareto efficiency** (finding win-wins)
- **Compensatory transfers** (side payments to losers)
- **Ethical reasoning** (whose interests count most?)

### Example Round Flow
1. Players declare preferences openly
2. Deliberation: "How do we balance these?"
3. Action selection: Vote on policy bundles
4. Consequence: Show impacts on each goal (multi-dimensional)
5. Debrief: "Was there a Pareto improvement we missed?"

### Advantages
- No deception (avoids "hidden objective guilt")
- Teaches value conflicts (core of real politics)
- Clear learning (see trade-offs explicitly)

### Challenges
- May feel abstract (no "win" condition)
- Requires philosophical maturity

## Variant 7: **Evolutionary Game (Iterated Play)**

### Core Mechanic
Players play multiple short games in sequence. Strategies **evolve** based on past performance.

### Structure
- **Each Session = 3 Rounds**: Quick games (15 min each)
- **Between Sessions**: Players can change strategies based on what worked
- **Population Dynamics**: If using multiple groups, successful strategies spread

### Learning Objectives
- **Evolutionary stability** (which strategies survive long-term)
- **Adaptation** (learning from failure)
- **Emergence** (meta-strategies evolve)

### Example
- Session 1: All players try different strategies
- Session 2: Players copy successful strategies from Session 1
- Session 3: Meta-strategy emerges ("always invest long-term")

## Variant 8: **AI Opponent Co-Player**

### Core Mechanic
One or more roles played by **AI agents** with programmed strategies.

### Structure
- Human players negotiate with AI agents
- AI has transparent utility function (can predict behavior)
- OR: AI is black-box (players must learn its strategy)

### Learning Objectives
- **Agent modeling** (predicting others)
- **Human-AI coordination**
- **Strategic reasoning**

## Hybrid Approaches

### Combination 1: **Temporal + Negotiation**
- Players negotiate over long-term investments
- Coalition must stay together across rounds
- Teaches commitment problems

### Combination 2: **Uncertainty + Mechanism Design**
- First half: Learn the system
- Second half: Design optimal mechanism given what you learned
- Teaches adaptive governance

### Combination 3: **Epistemic + Evolutionary**
- Each session, information distribution changes
- Players adapt to new info structure
- Teaches robustness

---

## Design Principles for Alternative TTX

### 1. **Clarity of Learning Objective**
What specifically should players learn? Design backward from that.

### 2. **Appropriate Complexity**
Match variant to audience sophistication.

### 3. **Feedback Richness**
Players must see consequences clearly to learn.

### 4. **Engagement Hooks**
- Temporal: "Will your investment pay off?"
- Negotiation: "Can you cut a deal?"
- Uncertainty: "Can you figure out the hidden rules?"

### 5. **Failure as Pedagogy**
Best learning often comes from failure. Design for instructive failure.

## Conclusion

The "personal vs public goal" tension is ONE pedagogical approach, but not the only one. Alternative variants can teach:

- **Information aggregation** (Variant 1)
- **Long-term thinking** (Variant 2)
- **Negotiation** (Variant 3)
- **Institution design** (Variant 4)
- **Learning under uncertainty** (Variant 5)
- **Value pluralism** (Variant 6)
- **Evolutionary dynamics** (Variant 7)

**Recommendation**: Implement 3-5 variants and let players/facilitators choose based on learning objectives and audience.

**Next Steps**:
1. Implement Variant 1 (easiest, most general)
2. Playtest with 20 participants
3. Compare to current design
4. Iterate
